Insurance websites / what I learned building one

What Medicare agentsneed to know beforebuilding a website.

The design was not the hardest part. The hard part was making sure the website did not create avoidable compliance, privacy or trust problems.

Steve Brooks
September 19, 2026

I learned this on a real project, not in a course.

When I started the Washington Benefits Group website, I expected the hard part to be organizing several insurance services without making the business feel cold or corporate. That mattered, but it was not the part that slowed me down.

The deeper work was figuring out which language had to be present, which questions the contact form should never ask, and what an older visitor needed to understand before trusting the site. That changed the entire build. Compliance was not something to paste into the footer at the end. It affected the footer, forms, service pages, frequently asked questions, local pages and contact language.

A necessary note

I am a web designer, not an attorney or compliance officer. This is not legal advice. Medicare rules change, carrier requirements differ and the agent or agency is responsible for final approval. A designer working in this field should know what to ask and where to verify it.

1. The Medicare disclaimer is not footer decoration

The first mistake would be copying a disclaimer from another insurance website and assuming it applies.

Federal rules define a third party marketing organization to include independent agents and brokers who are paid to take part in lead generation, marketing, sales or enrollment work for Medicare Advantage plans. The current rule requires the standardized disclaimer when a qualifying organization sells plans for more than one Medicare Advantage organization. A parallel rule covers Part D sponsors.

The required wording depends on whether the agent represents every organization in the service area. It also includes the number of organizations and products represented. That means the final language should come from current carrier or compliance guidance, not an old blog post or a competitor's footer.

The rule also says the disclaimer must be prominent on the website. Hiding it behind a privacy link or setting it in tiny gray type defeats the point. The same rule covers electronic communication and requires verbal delivery before a discussion of benefits during a sales call.

2. A contact form should know when to stop

A normal website inquiry does not need a Medicare number, Social Security number, payment information or a medical history. Asking for those details creates risk before the agent has even spoken with the person.

For the first conversation, the form usually needs a name, a safe way to respond, the state where the person lives, the general type of help requested and a preferred time to talk. Anything more should have a clear reason and an approved way to collect it.

I also put a plain warning next to insurance forms telling visitors not to send sensitive personal, medical or payment information. People do not always know what belongs in a message box. The website should tell them before they make a mistake.

If the business later needs protected information, that belongs in a properly reviewed secure process. A standard form that forwards everything into an ordinary email inbox is not that process.

3. State marketplace names are not decoration

Pennie® is Pennsylvania's official health insurance marketplace. It is not a generic name for private coverage, and its logo should not be used as a homemade trust badge.

Before publishing a state marketplace name, logo or certification claim, confirm the agent's current status and use the latest materials supplied by the marketplace or compliance team. The page should make the relationship clear without suggesting that the agency is the marketplace or a government office.

This matters even more when an agent serves several states. Each marketplace has its own language and brand material. One copied paragraph should not be stretched across every state page.

4. Google profile rules come before local search work

An agent without a walk in office should not show a home address or virtual office just to look more local.

Google says a business that visits or delivers to customers but does not serve them at its address should remove the address and use a service area profile. Google currently allows up to twenty service areas and says the overall boundary should generally stay within about two hours of the business.

That does not mean every remote insurance business automatically qualifies. Confirm that the business meets Google's eligibility rules and represents its real customer contact honestly. Do not invent a storefront, duplicate profiles across a state or list an office where nobody can meet a client.

5. Being licensed everywhere is not a search strategy

A home page that says licensed in all fifty states may be true, but it does not tell Google or a visitor where the agency has real experience, relationships and availability.

A useful site starts with the services people actually search for. Medicare guidance, individual health coverage, dental coverage, prescription coverage and help for self employed clients may each deserve their own page when the agency truly offers them.

Location pages should be just as honest. A Pennsylvania page and a Michigan page should not be the same paragraph with the state name swapped. Each page should explain who is served, how meetings work, which services apply and what makes the agency useful in that place.

The strongest local foundation is not a bag of keywords. It is a complete business profile, consistent contact information, real reviews, clear service pages, useful location pages and a website that answers the questions people ask before they call.

6. Design for the person actually using the site

A Medicare audience may include people dealing with changes in vision, hearing, dexterity or memory. The answer is not to make the website childish. The answer is to remove unnecessary difficulty.

Use readable text, strong contrast, clear headings, visible focus states, large tap targets and labels that do not disappear when someone starts typing. Keep the phone number easy to find and make it tappable. Explain errors in words. Do not make important information depend on a fast animation, a timed carousel or a tiny icon.

The writing matters too. Short sentences and clear labels are not dumbing anything down. They let a person spend attention on the insurance decision instead of figuring out the website.

7. The person building the site should ask uncomfortable questions

A designer does not need to become the agent's compliance department. The designer does need to notice when a decision could create a problem.

Before the layout begins, I would ask which Medicare products are actually sold, which carriers are represented, whether the business meets the federal definition of a third party marketing organization, who approves marketing language and how often the wording will be reviewed.

I would also ask which states are active priorities, whether clients can meet in person, what information the first contact form truly needs and who will receive those messages. Those answers affect the page structure, the disclaimer, the form, the local profile and the privacy language.

Checklist before an insurance website goes live

  • The current Medicare disclaimer has been confirmed by the agent, carriers or compliance contact.
  • The number of organizations and products in the disclaimer is accurate.
  • The disclaimer is easy to find and easy to read.
  • Contact forms collect only what the first conversation needs.
  • A warning tells visitors not to send sensitive personal, medical or payment information.
  • State marketplace names, marks and claims have been approved.
  • The Google profile matches how the business actually meets customers.
  • Service and location pages describe real work instead of repeating keywords.
  • Text, contrast, forms, navigation and tap targets have been checked for accessibility.
  • The privacy page explains how inquiry information is handled.
  • The sitemap, indexing and search tools are working.
  • Someone is responsible for reviewing rules and wording after launch.

The bottom line

A Medicare website should feel calm because the visitor already has enough to sort through. But calm design comes after the hard questions.

If a designer starts with colors before asking about plans, service areas, disclaimer language and form data, the order is backwards. The website can look polished and still create a problem the business never needed.

That was the biggest lesson from Washington Benefits Group. Research is part of the design. In a regulated field, it may be the most important part.
